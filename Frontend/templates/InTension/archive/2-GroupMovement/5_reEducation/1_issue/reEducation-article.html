Education-in-error

Education-in-error: the developmental form and content of the education system globally is structured toward tertiary education entrance (according to UNESCO this represents 40% of all people aged 18-24). The majority therefore can be argued to have been disadvantaged by this bias. Even more fundamental, the underpinning of education is based upon divisions of knowledge created during the European Enlightenment. These divisions directly link to professional practices and of education become dominantly instrumental and in the service of the economy. This modality of knowledge is at odds with the functional relational interconnectedness of Earth system and their ecologies. As a result, the way people learn to materially create, occupy and replicate the worlds of their existence as unsustainable as it negates the conditions that sustain life. To become futural the foundation of education needs to become so that people think and act relationally across all of their practices. While such a challenge is huge and daunting it is not a choice. Unless a process of adaptive change is made over coming decades our species, and many others, will not survive. This imperative adds to the state of tension everyone feels, as such it has to be a major issue of this publication’s agenda. The picture of the energy uptake and emission created by A! is still unclear, in part because its corporate developers and producers are not disclosing data. But what is clear from Technology Review’s series MIT ‘Power Hungry: AI and our energy future[1],’ is that its impacts are significant and will grow, and that the lack of information is generating misinformation. However, the MIT review , drawing on multiple sources, makes clear ‘the energy resources required to power this artificial-intelligence revolution are staggering, and the world’s biggest tech companies have made it a top priority to harness ever more of that energy, aiming to reshape our energy grids in the process.’ The machine learning that provides data that the AI information system runs on takes a huge amount of computer time and therefore uses a lot of energy. The picture the MIT review presents is of ‘racks of servers humming along for months, ingesting training data, crunching numbers, and performing computations.’ They also need energy to power other network equipment, lighting and cooling. In 2025 the US Trump Government announced a $500 billion investment to build ten new data centres (around fifty percent of their energy load being created by AI. AI data centres require constant power, 24-7, 365 days a year. Citing Rahul Mewawalla, the CEO of Mawson Infrastructure Group, which builds and maintains high-energy data centre, the review notes that the data centres can’t rely on intermittent technologies like wind and solar power, and on average, they tend to use dirtier electricity. User uptake of AI makes major energy demands upon data centres, and both the users and centres can be anywhere in the world—the is no necessary correlation between the user’s national location and that of the data centre. Corporations are of course building centres in nations where energy is cheap as fossil-fuel generated. All indications are that the use of AI is going to increase for a considerable time, and obviously with it a corresponding increase of energy. There are no current indicators when this demand would peek.
